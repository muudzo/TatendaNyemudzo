## Context

In Zimbabwe, cattle are a savings account. People store and move value in livestock when they
don't trust the currency, and they buy and sell it at physical auctions: one place, one day a
week, a room full of dealers.

Paynow, the country's largest payment gateway, gave me a six-week internship with two jobs:
build a working livestock auction marketplace, and use it to measure how Paynow's developer
experience compares with regional and international payment providers.

## Problem

Online livestock trade already exists, on Facebook Marketplace and WhatsApp groups. It's cheap
and it's risky: no escrow, no verification, a high no-show rate. The auction house is the
opposite: trustworthy, and expensive in ways you only see from the inside.

So the question was never "can we put an auction online?" It was: **what does the auction
house actually sell, and can a phone provide it for less?**

## Thinking

Before designing anything I attended two physical auctions, on 19 and 25 March. My notes from the
first visit became the product's foundation.

<Note kind="evidence">
  Every buyer pays a **US$1,000 deposit** before they can bid. Average monthly income is around
  US$200–300.
</Note>

The first thing I saw was the gate. A US$1,000 deposit keeps out unserious bidders, and it also
keeps out every smallholder who wants one goat for US$80.

The second was the fee. The house takes 5% from the seller and 7% from the buyer: **12% of every
sale**, before transport and police clearance.

| On a US$1,200 sale | Fee | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Seller | 5% | −US$60 |
| Buyer | 7% | +US$84 |
| **House** | **12%** | **US$144** |

The third was speed. The auctioneer talks fast on purpose, because urgency pushes prices up. The
fourth was who *wasn't* there on a Wednesday: anyone with a job, rural smallholders outside the
catchment, the diaspora buying for family at home. That became the wedge.

<SignaturePlate slug="zimlivestock" />

The last observation turned into the product's most useful number. The highest bid card I saw was
**45**, in a room of roughly 200. Most people were watching, not bidding. A marketplace doesn't
have to fill a room. It needs about 45 serious bidders per listing. My second visit turned this
into a metric, *Active Bidders per Listing*, which is the leading indicator of whether an
auction is economically viable.

<Note kind="decision">
  Don't compete with Facebook on price. Offer **auction-level trust at half the fee**.
</Note>

Facebook prices are lower because Facebook carries the risk. Matching them would mean giving up
the only thing that makes the auction house worth 12%.

<Note kind="rejected">
  Building a logistics service. Two independent truckers at the auction were already cold-calling
  farmers. Start by introducing them manually over WhatsApp; no code until demand is proven.
</Note>

Transport and police clearance are both real pain points. I deliberately didn't solve either at
launch. Clearance stays physical, as the law requires; the listing asks for a photo of the stock
card and brand mark so the buyer knows what to expect.

## Design

I mapped everything the auction house provides to its digital equivalent, including the
uncomfortable parts:

| The auction house | ZimLivestock |
|---|---|
| US$1,000 deposit | US$50–100 refundable escrow via Paynow |
| 12% total fees | 5–6% total, shown line by line at checkout |
| Physical inspection | Video listings, stock-card photos, seller history |
| A room full of people | "People watching" counts and live bid updates |
| Fast auctioneer | A 90-second countdown that matches the room's cadence |
| Wednesday, one place | Any day, anywhere with a phone |

The 90-second countdown is a compromise. Asynchronous bidding gives buyers time to think, which
probably means lower final prices. That's good for buyers and a harder sell to sellers. I wrote that
down rather than hiding it: sellers may get slightly less per animal but keep more after fees.

I designed 12 interactive wireframes and a system-flow diagram before building the flow from
listing to bid to win to payment.

## Build

The stack is a React 18 PWA on Supabase: Postgres with row-level security, Realtime for live
bids, and 18+ Deno edge functions for payments, agents and QA.

The parts I care about most are the ones nobody sees:

- **Bids go through one door.** A `place_bid` database function locks the listing's row and
  checks every rule (amount, reserve, status, end time, no self-bidding) before inserting. There
  is no other way to create a bid.
- **Exactly once.** Bids and payments carry `crypto.randomUUID()` idempotency keys, enforced by
  partial unique indexes. A double-tap on a slow connection can't bid or pay twice.
- **Payments fall back.** An orchestrator retries in order: EcoCash, then OneMoney, then card.
  A settlement ledger records what actually cleared.
- **Production polices itself.** Every merge to `main` runs a consistency checker, a security
  agent and a chaos test *against production*. Any hard failure turns CI red, with no override.

To benchmark providers honestly, I integrated all five into the same codebase rather than
comparing documentation. Each has its own checkout function wired through the same payment path.

## Iteration

Four changes came straight from the field notes:

| What I saw | What changed |
|---|---|
| A US$1,000 deposit prices most buyers out | Escrow holds of US$50–100 (schema ready) |
| Hidden 12% fees erode trust | A line-by-line fee breakdown at checkout |
| Buyers don't trust a listing without proof | Stock-card photo upload in the seller flow |
| Sellers want a trust signal | Payment-history trust badges |

The more important iteration came from the QA gate. On 13 April the post-deploy audit found three
severity-1 problems, all fixed the same day:

<Note kind="evidence">
  `bf3da08` · `921bc62` · `4cd7109`. Each fix is its own commit, linked from the audit report.
</Note>

1. **Anyone could win any auction for $1.** A permissive insert policy let an authenticated user
   write straight to the bids table, skipping `place_bid`. I removed it; bids now have one door.
2. **CORS fell back to `*`.** Seven edge functions used `|| "*"` when an environment variable
   was missing. Now they fail closed.
3. **Search was broken past the first page.** It filtered on the client, page by page. It moved
   to the server.

The first one is why I build the gate before the features. My own tests had passed. The system
that attacked production is what caught it.

## Result

- **A working marketplace in production**: list, bid, win, pay, installable as a PWA.
- **A ranked developer-experience report** across five providers, with seven recommendations.
  Stripe scored 9.7/10, Paystack 8.0, Flutterwave 7.2, Paynow 4.2, and Pesepay 3.8 (blocked by
  malformed HTTP headers).
- **The most valuable finding was the blocker itself.** Paynow's API sits behind Cloudflare bot
  protection on its main domain, so *no server-side code can call it*: no edge function, no
  Lambda, not even `curl`. Browsers pass the challenge; servers can't. Until that changes, Paynow
  can't be used from modern cloud architectures.
- A second comparison found that two of Paynow's own sibling products, BillPay and TXT, already
  use the fixes Paynow Core needs: a separate API subdomain, Basic Auth, documented test
  prefixes. The fix wasn't research. It was adopting patterns the company already had.

<Note kind="open">
  At handover, 12 of 15 success criteria were green. Live Paynow payments were blocked by
  Cloudflare, user feedback was about 30% collected, and the stakeholder demos were still
  scheduled.
</Note>

## Reflection

The field visits were worth more than any feature. One Wednesday in an auction room gave me
the deposit, the fee, the wedge and the liquidity target. I would have designed a different, and
worse, product from a desk.

What I'd change: I built the engineering safety net early and the user-feedback loop late. The
QA gate caught a bug that would have lost real money. But by the end I'd heard from more
automated agents than farmers. Next time, the first stakeholder session happens in the same week
as the first deploy.

What it says about how I work: I like finding the one rule a system can enforce ("bids have one
door", "fees are always shown") and then making it impossible to break rather than unlikely.
