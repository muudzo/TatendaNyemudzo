## Context

Zimbabwe's national railway is being rebuilt, and it needs a way to take fares. The reference model
is Taiwan's EasyCard. Local conditions are different, though: mobile money dominates, few people
have bank cards, connectivity is patchy, power cuts are scheduled, and there are two currencies
(USD and ZiG) whose exchange rate moves.

## Decisions

Most fare systems today are *account-based*: the gate asks a server whether you've paid. That
fails the moment the network or the grid does, and here both fail regularly.

<Note kind="decision">
  **Card first.** A stored-value smart card is the source of truth for its own balance, protected
  by cryptography. Mobile money, QR and bank cards are online conveniences layered on top.
</Note>

<Note kind="rejected">
  Mobile-money-first and QR-only fail during outages. Tap-your-bank-card fails on card
  penetration. Both are kept, but not as the foundation.
</Note>

One card handles both fare models. Commuter lines charge tap-on, tap-off. Intercity trains carry a
reserved-seat ticket loaded onto the card. The gate decides which applies by what it finds on the
card. Currency policy (hold value in USD to shield riders from ZiG swings?) sits behind
configuration. It's a regulator's decision, not a code change.

## Build

<Plate label="Key hierarchy" caption="Gates hold the key that takes money. Only top-up devices hold the key that adds it. A stolen gate can’t mint value.">
  <pre class="plate-pre">Master issuer key        (HSM, never leaves the data centre)
        │ diversified per card
        ▼
Per-card keys ── App master   (issuance only)
              ├─ Debit        (gate SAMs)
              ├─ Credit/load  (top-up SAMs only)
              └─ Read/log     (inspector handhelds)</pre>
  <p slot="text">One master issuer key lives in a hardware security module. Keys are derived for each card from it. Gate readers hold only the debit key, top-up devices hold the credit key, and inspectors can only read. Compromising a gate exposes one revocable module, never the master key.</p>
</Plate>

The whole tap (mutual authentication, reading entry state, checking the deny-list, computing
the fare, an atomic debit that the card itself refuses if it would go negative) happens between
the card and the reader. The central system is never in the tap path. Transactions are signed,
queued, and sent on when the connection returns.

## Where it stands

This is Phase 0: requirements, a locked scope in an architecture decision record, and the card
core designed in detail. **There is no application code, deliberately.** The next steps are
regulatory conversations (RBZ, POTRAZ) and a build-versus-buy decision on card cryptography,
which you buy rather than build.

It's in this portfolio because it's the clearest example of how I think: start from what fails,
put the source of truth where the failure can't reach it, and write the reasons down.
