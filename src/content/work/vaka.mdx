## Context

*Vaka* is Shona for "to build". In Zimbabwe, building control belongs to local authorities:
32 urban councils and around 60 rural ones, working under model building by-laws. If you're
building a house, the council inspects it five times, from foundation to final sign-off,
and you pay a fee at each stage.

Today each stage means booking an inspector, waiting for a site visit, getting a paper card
signed, and paying, often in cash.

## Problem

That process fails in three ways at once:

1. **Paper per stage.** Files get lost, nobody can see status, and delays run to weeks. Local
   guidance puts the foundation and final inspections at around a month each.
2. **Cash per stage.** Fees leak before they reach the council's books. There's no audit trail,
   and it exposes inspectors to bribery.
3. **One inspector, many offices.** Travel, scheduling chaos, missed visits.

Behind all three is the real problem: **the order of stages is only a convention.** Nothing stops
a slab going down before the foundation is passed, or a fee being paid to the wrong pocket.

## Thinking

I started with the by-laws and the council guidance, and wrote a sourced research dossier before
any code. Every figure I couldn't confirm is marked `[validate]` and stays out of the pitch until
it's checked.

<Note kind="evidence">
  Harare and Bulawayo already accept Paynow for rates, fees and levies, via BillPay and council ERP
  vendors.
</Note>

That finding changed the product. I had assumed digital payment was the innovation. It isn't:
councils already take mobile money for bills. What *none* of them have is the workflow in between:
stage gating, offline inspector sign-off, builder verification, a reconciled audit log.

<Note kind="decision">
  Vaka is the **orchestration layer**, not a payments competitor. It sits on the rail councils
  already trust and holds no money itself. That also keeps it out of money-transmission
  licensing.
</Note>

I also looked for the route into government, because "African government sales cycles are
infinite" is the first objection anyone raises. The ZIDA Act (2020) accepts *unsolicited*
public-private partnership proposals, with a preliminary assessment within 14 days. Nineteen of
the 26 PPP proposals ZIDA received since late 2023 were unsolicited. There's a real door.

## Design

The core of the design is one rule:

> A stage unlocks only after the previous stage is **paid** on Paynow and **passed** by an
> inspector on site.

<SignaturePlate slug="vaka" />

Every stage moves through an explicit state machine:
`LOCKED → AWAITING_PAYMENT → PAID_AWAITING_INSPECTION → BOOKED → INSPECTED_PASS | INSPECTED_FAIL`.
A fail returns the stage to remediation. Only a pass opens the next gate.

The same engine serves four people, each with a different surface:

| Persona | Their surface | The one thing they need |
|---|---|---|
| Applicant / builder | Web (later USSD, WhatsApp) | "What do I do next?" One clear action per screen |
| Inspector | Mobile, offline-first | Today's nearby jobs; photo + GPS + pass/fail |
| Council clerk | Dashboard | Fees reconciled, inspector load, audit trail |
| Ministry | Read-only overview | The national picture |

A certificate of occupation is issued as a QR code that anyone can verify at a public URL without
logging in. A forged paper certificate is easy to make; a forged record in the registry is not.

I built a small design system, *Civic Confidence*, for this. Government software is usually
either stark or decorative, and this needed to feel like an institution you can trust with
money, while staying plain enough for a builder on a phone in the sun.

## Build

<Note kind="decision">
  NestJS + Prisma + Postgres over Supabase. A government buyer needs data residency and
  self-hosting on Zimbabwean infrastructure. A hosted backend was the wrong trade here, although
  it was the right one for ZimLivestock.
</Note>

- **Guards, pipes, interceptors.** Role-based access on every endpoint; input validated at the
  boundary, with unknown fields rejected; an interceptor writes an **append-only audit entry** for
  every money or state transition.
- **Atomic gates.** Recording an inspection and changing a stage's status commit together, or not
  at all.
- **Payments server-side only.** Without credentials it runs in simulation mode, so the demo
  needs no keys. With keys it sends a real EcoCash prompt to the applicant's phone and polls to
  settle. The demo shortcut is disabled in live mode.
- **Fails closed.** In production it refuses to start with weak or default secrets. Auth uses
  argon2id, short-lived access tokens held in memory, and httpOnly refresh cookies.

I built it with AI coding agents working under a written contract: a `CLAUDE.md` that holds the
domain glossary, the security checklist and the non-negotiables, plus a `CONTRACT.md` for the API
and design. I write the decisions and constraints, and the agents work inside them.

## Iteration

The biggest change happened on paper, before the build. Positioning moved from "digital
payments for councils" to "the inspection workflow on top of the payments councils already
take". That removed a competitor (Paynow and BillPay) and turned it into infrastructure.

The second change was scope. The first plan digitised everything: plan scrutiny, multi-council
tenancy, live CIFOZ and ZIMRA lookups. I cut it to the **inspection → payment → sign-off loop**,
because that's where the pain and the return on investment are sharpest.

## Result

A working prototype with seeded Bulawayo data and a three-minute demo: the applicant pays for a
stage, the inspector signs it off with photo and GPS, the next stage unlocks, and the council
sees the fee already reconciled with a full audit trail.

<Note kind="open">
  No council has used it yet. Permit volumes and fee schedules for a first council are still
  marked `[validate]`. The next step is a no-cost pilot agreement with one council.
</Note>

## Reflection

This is the project where I understood that the most valuable design decision can be a
positioning decision. The research didn't change a screen; it changed *what the product is*.

It also let me act on what ZimLivestock taught me. There, a single missing rule meant someone
could win an auction for $1. Here I designed the rule first and let the screens follow from it.

What I'd do differently: I should have talked to an inspector before designing the inspector's
app. I know the process from documents. I don't yet know the day.
